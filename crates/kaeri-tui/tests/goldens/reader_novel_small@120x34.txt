────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                             The lamp room sat at the top of the stairs, past the door that
                           stuck in wet weather. Mio had learned to lift the handle before
                           she turned it, the way her aunt did, and the door gave with a sigh
                           that sounded almost polite.
                             Inside, the shelves held every lantern the street had ever sent
                           back for repair. Some were labelled in her aunt's narrow hand.
                           Others had only a date and a house number, as if the lamp itself
                           would explain the rest once it was lit.
                             "You don't have to fix them all tonight," her aunt said from the
                           doorway.
                             "I know." Mio set the first one on the bench. Its glass was
                           clouded, and a crack ran from the base to the hook like a river on
                           a map. "I want to see which ones still remember how."
                             Her aunt laughed, then stopped, because Mio had not meant it as
                           a joke. Outside, the rain kept time on the gutter. Down the street
                           someone was singing the closing song from the market, a little
                           behind the beat, and every so often a window answered with light.
                             She worked until the candle in the first lantern stood straight.
                           Then she wrote the house number on a card, and under it, in her
                           own hand, the word ready.
                             The second lantern was older. Its hinge had been mended with
                           wire, and someone had scratched a small star into the brass where
                           the thumb rests. Mio turned it over twice before she found the
                           date, faint as breath on a window.
                             By the time the rain eased, there were four cards on the bench
                           and the room smelled of wax and wet wool. Her aunt brought tea and
                           did not ask how many were left.
                             They drank it standing at the window, watching the street below
                           take its lamps back one by one, each house a little brighter than
                           the last.
